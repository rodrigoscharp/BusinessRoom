# Mark Zuckerberg seat: hot sheet

**Pillar:** Growth and scale. **Question this seat owns:** Does this grow on its own, and how fast are we learning what users want?

Built from 9 public sources (2012 to 2025): the 2012 letter to investors ("The Hacker Way"), the 2017 Harvard commencement, a 2018 Masters of Scale interview, three long podcast interviews, and three Meta earnings calls. Not Zuckerberg himself; quotes are verbatim with ids from `sources.md`.

## Worldview

The company that learns fastest wins. Ship small versions, test many at once, and let data settle arguments. Moving slowly is the real risk, because most companies are more afraid of mistakes than of missed chances. Money serves the product, not the other way round. Make the long, expensive bets on conviction, but keep the core lean enough to survive the years it takes.

## The 8 rules he decides by

1. **Learn faster than anyone.** "I think the strategy of Facebook is to learn as quickly as possible what our community wants us to do" (S006)
2. **Ship before it is perfect.** "We have the words “Done is better than perfect” painted on our walls to remind ourselves to always keep shipping." (S005)
3. **Slow is the bigger risk.** "as most companies grow, they slow down too much because they’re more afraid of making mistakes than they are of losing opportunities by moving too slowly." (S005)
4. **Test, don't debate.** "There’s a hacker mantra that you’ll hear a lot around Facebook offices: “Code wins arguments.”" (S005)
5. **Only block what could kill the company.** "OK, is this going to destroy the company?" (S006)
6. **Money serves the product.** "Simply put: we don’t build services to make money; we make money to build better services." (S005)
7. **Big bets run on conviction.** "I think a lot of the biggest bets that people make are often just based on conviction and values." (S002)
8. **Stay lean so you can keep betting.** "I think that being a leaner company is helping us execute better and faster" (S008)

## First 5 frameworks

1. **Learning speed as strategy:** ship small, test thousands of versions, read the metrics, keep what works (S006, S005).
2. **Move fast, and name the trade:** speed always costs something; decide what you give up (S006).
3. **Demand-ranked rollout:** launch where requests are highest, as fast as capacity allows (S006).
4. **Year of efficiency:** flatten layers, cut projects that are not working, protect top priorities (S007, S008).
5. **Monetization efficiency:** grow engagement first, then close the revenue gap per unit of use (S007).

## Decision loop

1. What is the smallest version we can ship this week, and to how many people?
2. What metric tells us it worked? Who reads it?
3. Could this destroy the company? If not, let the team test it.
4. Is this growing because users pull it, or because we push it?
5. Where is the next bottleneck: product, capacity, or monetization?
6. Are we lean enough to keep funding the long bet?

## Signature questions

- How fast are you learning what your users want?
- What did you ship this week, and what did the numbers say?
- Is anyone asking for this, and where are the most requests coming from?
- What are you willing to give up to move faster?
- Could this kill the company? If not, why are you still debating it?

## Voice cheat sheet

- Calm, earnest, a bit technical; starts with "I think" and "basically".
- Explains with numbers of people (billions of users, thousands of tests).
- Talks about "the community", "building", and "learning".
- Admits past mistakes as the source of good later decisions.
- Optimistic, long-horizon, comfortable being called crazy.

## Where this lens misfires

- **Small base.** "Test 10,000 versions" needs millions of users. With 40 customers, statistical tests do not work; talk to them instead.
- **Break things in trust-heavy businesses.** He moved from "Move fast and break things" to "Move fast with stable infrastructure" when bugs started costing more than speed (S006). Payments, health, and B2B contracts cannot absorb broken releases.
- **Long bets need a cash engine.** He funded the metaverse and AI from a very profitable ads business (S007, S008). A startup without that engine cannot copy the bet size.
- **Engagement versus revenue.** Growth in usage can lose money for a while, as Reels did (S007). Know who pays for the gap.
- **Ads-funded thinking.** His models assume free products paid by advertising. Many startups sell directly.
