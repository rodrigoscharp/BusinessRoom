# Round 1: opening positions

## Graham (weight 3)

SEAT: GRAHAM (Product and startups)
STANCE: Conditional yes
OPTION SCORES: A 2/10 · B 7/10 · C 6/10
PILLAR SCORE: 6/10 · CONFIDENCE: 4/5 · RUIN RISK: none · DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Take B, the $1M SAFE, but spend it only on getting the CEO off sales calls and on cutting churn, and keep the company at or near profitable.

WHY:
1. Startup = Growth (S100). 4% a month works out to 1.6x a year, which is about 1% a week, and that is slow. The VC wants 3x in 18 months, which takes 6.3% a month. All of today's growth comes from word of mouth and one Facebook group, and I see no evidence that money turns into growth here. Option A signs them up for a number they have no machine to hit.
2. Default alive (S080). They're already there. "Once you cross the threshold of profitability, however low, your runway becomes infinite." (S145) That's the best position to raise from, because they don't need it. Taking $4M with a 3x mandate is how you end up hiring ahead of demand, and "Hiring too fast is by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money." (S080)
3. Equity equation (S169). B gives up 8%, so it has to lift the outcome by about 9%. If freeing the CEO moves growth from 4% to 5% a month, the five-year multiple goes from 10.5x to 18.7x, which clears 9% easily. A gives up 20% and needs a 25% lift, and it has a second problem. At a $20M post, a $50M exit only returns the VC 2.5x, so the board will push for an outcome the founders have said they don't need. On the competitors: "Competitors are rarely what kills startups. Poor execution does." (S056) Neither competitor serves shops under 10 technicians.

C is close behind. Its weak spot is that 4% a month for five years gets MRR to about $420k, roughly $5M ARR, which probably falls short of $50M. Growth rate decides this, so some money pointed at growth beats none.

THE RISK I SEE: 2.5% monthly logo churn means losing about 26% of customers a year. Any money spent on acquisition goes into a leaky bucket.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If a hired rep (not the CEO) can push net growth past 6% a month within a 60-day test, the 3x becomes reachable and I'd look at A again.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Call every customer who churned in the last 90 days (about 15) and find out why they left.
QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: What would the first $500k of the $4M go to, and what evidence do you have that it raises the growth rate?

## Buffett (weight 3)

SEAT: BUFFETT (Money and capital)
STANCE: Conditional yes
OPTION SCORES: A 3/10 · B 7/10 · C 6/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10 · CONFIDENCE: 3/5 · RUIN RISK: none · DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: I'd take B, the $1M SAFE, and spend it on two things: a closer so the CEO stops carrying all the sales, and cutting churn. I wouldn't spend it on a race.

WHY:
1. By my ranking this is already a great business. It grows about 60% a year with an 82% gross margin, it makes a profit, and growth uses almost no capital (Great, good, and gruesome, S080). Forcing $4M through it risks turning See's into an airline.
2. Here is A's cash math. At 4% a month you reach about $81k MRR in 18 months. Tripling to $120k takes 6.3% a month, which works out to roughly 250 extra customers. Say $3.6M goes to buying them (my assumption). That's about $14k a customer against lifetime gross profit of about $6,600: $164 a month over a 40-month life at 2.5% churn. "Some companies will turn these retained dollars into fifty-cent pieces, others into two-dollar bills." (S083)
3. A's price is about 42x ARR, so it passes the issuance test on paper (S055). The real cost is the board seat and the fund math. A $50M exit would thrill these founders, but it disappoints a VC who bought in at $20M post, so that board seat will push for a bigger swing than the owners want. "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut." (S082) B still gets about 25x ARR for 8%, and nobody gets a hand on the plug. My lens is known to misfire on raising money, so this is the issuance test applied to these numbers. It's not a blanket no on outside money.

THE RISK I SEE: One founder working 65 hours a week is the whole sales engine. If he breaks, B and C stall alike (Noah's Law, S088).
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A tested paid channel with customer acquisition cost under $2,000 and payback under 12 months, holding up at $100k a month of spend. Then A's dollars would earn more than a dollar, and I'd move.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Write A's 18-month plan on one page (hires, burn, and cost per new customer) and check it against the $6,600 a customer is worth.
QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: If you take A and you're at 2x with $1M left in month 15, who decides what happens next?

## Bezos (weight 2)

SEAT: BEZOS (Strategy and decisions)
STANCE: Yes
OPTION SCORES: A 3/10 · B 8/10 · C 6/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10 · CONFIDENCE: 4/5 · RUIN RISK: none · DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Take B, the $1M from operator angels, and spend it on getting the CEO off every sales call and bringing churn down.

WHY:
1. I sort the door first (Type 1 and Type 2, S063). A is a one-way door. The cash is easy to spend, but the board seat and the 3x plan are hard to give back. At $20M post, a $50M sale returns the VC 2.5x, which is a weak result for a fund, so the board will lean against the very outcome you say you want. B costs 8% and leaves the decisions with you.
2. The 3x plan doesn't fit this business. At 4% a month you get 2.0x in 18 months, and 3x needs about 6.3% a month with no sales team. Missing a board's plan while carrying $4M of payroll is how a profitable company turns desperate. "I don't believe in bet the company bets that's when you're desperate" (S018).
3. Early capital should take risks off the table (systematic risk elimination, S008). Your two biggest risks are a CEO who runs every sale and 2.5% monthly churn, which is about 26% of customers a year. I'm assuming a seller and an onboarding hire cost about $300k a year all-in, so $1M pays for three years of work on exactly those two problems.
4. Work backwards from the customer (S056). Ten years from now a six-truck plumber will still want fewer missed jobs and fast support, and the funded rivals don't serve him. "Don't be afraid of our competitors, because they're never going to send us any money. Be afraid of our customers." (S047)

C can work too. You're default alive, so a long clock is safe here, which is rare for a company this size. The problem with C is that the CEO stays the whole sales engine.

THE RISK I SEE: The angel money goes into general headcount, churn stays at 2.5%, and you pour fuel into a leaking tank.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A 90-day test where a hired seller pushes monthly growth above 6.5% with CAC payback under 12 months. Then A is a fair bet, and at a better price than $20M.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Ask the five angels to close, and post the sales role before the CEO's next demo.
QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: If someone offers $50M in year three, will you take it, and would your VC let you?

## Hormozi (weight 2)

SEAT: HORMOZI (Offers, pricing, and sales)
STANCE: Yes
OPTION SCORES: A 2/10 · B 5/10 · C 8/10
PILLAR SCORE: 6/10 · CONFIDENCE: 3/5 · RUIN RISK: none · DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Turn down A, run C with a 90-day money model fix (annual prepay, a setup fee, a price test on new customers, and one hired closer), and keep the angels warm as a fallback.

WHY:
1. LTV to CAC (S070). Gross profit is $164 a month per customer. At 2.5% churn a customer stays about 40 months, so lifetime gross profit is about $6,560. People staff sales and onboarding here, so the floor sits nearer 6:1 than 3:1 (S089), which caps CAC at about $1,100. Nobody has measured CAC on a single paid channel. A means putting $4M into an acquisition engine that has never been tested.
2. Client-financed acquisition (S037): "30-day cash must be greater than two times the cost of acquiring customer and the cost of fulfilling that customer." Monthly billing collects $200 in the first 30 days, which fails at any real CAC. Annual billing at ten months' price ($2,000) plus a $500 setup fee collects $2,500 up front. If 60 of the 200 current customers switch to annual, that's $120k in cash, two-thirds of what's in the bank, with zero dilution.
3. Constraint first (S113). The constraint is sales: one founder working 65 hours and taking every call. Money doesn't fix that. A closer and a better offer do. Hitting 3x in 18 months needs about 6.3% monthly growth, roughly 32 new customers a month against about 13 today. Retention is already fine: "Churn under 3%. That's what you want." (S113)

THE RISK I SEE: The founder stays the only closer and the money model changes never ship. C then becomes 4% a month forever while the funded rivals move down-market.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A tested channel showing CAC under $1,100, payback under 6 months, and 30+ new customers a month. At that point outside money is fuel, and B (or even A) scores much higher.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Offer every current customer annual prepay as "buy 10, get 2" (S016) and count the yeses before the term sheet deadline.
QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: What's your close rate on sales calls, and what does one new customer cost you all in? If it's above 80%, your $200 price is too low.

Assumptions: CAC, close rate, and gross new adds are not in the brief. I took gross adds as about 13 a month: 8 net from 4% growth plus 5 lost to churn.

## Martell (weight 2)

SEAT: MARTELL (People, hiring, and founder time)
STANCE: Yes
OPTION SCORES: A 4/10 · B 8/10 · C 5/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10 · CONFIDENCE: 3/5 · RUIN RISK: none · DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Take B, and commit the first $250k to the Replacement Ladder in order: an assistant, then an onboarding owner, then a closer.

WHY:
1. Buyback Rate (S018). I'm assuming the CEO takes $120k plus half of the $72k profit. That's $156k over 2,000 hours, divided by 4, so about $20 an hour. An offshore assistant costs $1,500 a month for 160 hours (S066), which is under $10 an hour. Every inbox and scheduling hour he keeps is money lost.
2. Replacement Ladder (S031). Nobody holds the admin rung, and he still runs most onboarding himself. I'm assuming his 65 hours split into 25 for sales, 20 for onboarding, and 20 for admin and everything else. An assistant plus an onboarding owner frees about 25 hours. He puts those hours into sales and the Facebook group (Buyback Loop, S020). Once sales takes half his week, he hires a closer trained on 20 recorded calls (Camcorder Method, S020).
3. Pain Line (S020). "Entrepreneurs will not grow into pain." (S014) Option A asks for 3x in 18 months. That means about 6.3% growth a month against 4% today. I'm assuming roughly 15 hires plus a board seat. At about 20 founder hours per tested hire and 3 board hours a week, A adds around 7 hours to a 65-hour week before it frees a single one. B pays for the ladder without that deadline. C can pay for the assistant out of profit, but it can't carry the onboarding owner and the closer together, and "You should not be capital constraint." (S017)

THE RISK I SEE: They spend the $1M on capacity, like two more engineers or a VP of Sales, while the CEO still runs onboarding at 65 hours. Or they hand sales to a rep before scripts exist, and the rep fails inside six months (S031).
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: A two-week audit showing he already turns away 20 or more qualified demos a month. In that case demand is bigger than capacity, and A's $4M would have work to do right away.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Start the two-week Time and Energy Audit on Monday, logging every 15 minutes, and record every sales and onboarding call as it happens.
QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Would you still want the $4M if you worked 45 hours a week and someone else owned onboarding?

## Wickman (weight 2)

SEAT: WICKMAN (Operations and scaling)
STANCE: Conditional yes
OPTION SCORES: A 2/10 · B 7/10 · C 5/10
PILLAR SCORE: 6/10 · CONFIDENCE: 3/5 · RUIN RISK: none · DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: I'd take B, the $1M SAFE, but only after both co-founders sign one V/TO and only if the money goes to two seats that get the CEO out of sales and onboarding.

WHY:
1. The root problem here is People, and more capital won't fix it (Six Key Components, S043). Right now the CEO is the Visionary, the whole sales seat, and most of the onboarding seat, at 65 hours a week. Option A pours $4M into an Accountability Chart where one person holds three seats. "We always teach structure first, people second." (S044)
2. Option A's math breaks a six-person team. Going 3x in 18 months means MRR moves from $40k to $120k, which is about 6.3% net growth every month. At the current 4% (1.04^18), they land near $81k. With 2.5% churn on top, they'd need about 8.8% gross adds every month, and today every lead comes from one Facebook group and one founder's calendar. Getting there takes a sales team, an Integrator, roughly 15 to 20 people, and a board, all in 18 months. Board pressure also pulls the company up toward bigger shops and out of the under-10-technician Core Focus, which is the one market neither competitor serves (Core Focus, S110).
3. The co-founders are split, with one on A and one on C. That's a Vision problem, and you can't settle it in a three-week reply window. "Is your vision out of your brain in a document where your leadership team is crystal clear, 100% on the same page." (S066) With all due love and respect to the CEO, "competitors with funding will outspend us" is a decision made out of fear (Three Discoveries, S031).
4. B passes the stress test (Reverse Accountability Chart, S098). Monthly costs run about $34k, so $180k covers roughly 5.3 months, which is just short of six. B adds $1M. Two hires at about $25k a month combined still leave more than 24 months of runway, even if revenue drops 50%. C leaves the CEO as the bottleneck, with only $6k a month of profit to fix that.

THE RISK I SEE: The money lands and the CEO still takes every sales call, so we've just bought a more expensive bottleneck.
WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If a non-founder already closes at least half of new deals and the CEO is under 50 hours a week, I'd move A up to a 6.
WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Get both co-founders out of the office for one full day to draw the Accountability Chart for 18 months out and put names in the seats before anyone replies to the VC. Stay focused.
QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Who is your Integrator, and is it either of you?

3. **What does the money buy first?** Martell: assistant, then onboarding owner, then closer (Replacement Ladder). Hormozi and Buffett: a closer. Graham and Bezos: closer plus churn. Wickman: nothing until both co-founders sign one V/TO and draw the Accountability Chart.
